About

I am a PhD candidate at UC Irvine, in Biomedical Engineering and Radiological Sciences. I work on foundation models and multimodal learning, usually where the data is small and the evaluation is the hard part.

Most of my work starts from the same constraint. Clinical datasets are limited, and the thing worth predicting, how a tumor is actually behaving, sits inside a biopsy. Imaging is the opposite: non-invasive, repeatable, and already part of standard care, but it shows anatomy rather than biology. My PhD is about closing that gap with models pretrained elsewhere, so that the biology is what gets learned here, not the representation.

The same problem turns up outside medicine. Training a vision-language model with reinforcement learning, I watched it stop reasoning out loud. The reward was exact-match accuracy, reasoning made the phrasing drift, and saying less scored better. The model had read the incentive correctly and the incentive was wrong: auditing the grader showed that between a quarter and half of the answers it marked wrong were right.

Both lines of work arrive at the same place. A number is worth what you checked it against, which is why the medical models are held against stained tissue and the language models against a second reader.

Before the PhD I spent two years as an NLP engineer at Tencent QTrade, building named-entity models for a brokerage chat platform with more than 500,000 daily users. That is where I learned what a wrong prediction costs once it is serving live traffic. Away from research I photograph landscapes, mostly in the American West.

Reading tumor biology from a scan the patient already gets

Cross-species study of pancreatic cancer, mouse 9.4T MRI and human clinical CT.  Under review

Pancreatic cancer kills most patients within five years, and what separates one prognosis from another is largely the tumor's surroundings: how dense the scar tissue is, how many immune cells got through it. Reading that today requires a biopsy, which is invasive, samples one spot, and cannot be repeated often. A CT or MRI scan has none of those problems, and every patient already gets one. It just does not, on its own, say anything about the biology.

Preclinical and clinical imaging cohorts are small, a few dozen subjects being ordinary rather than exceptional, and that is precisely where fine-tuning a large network overfits. What works instead is to leave the pretrained vision model frozen and fit a small classical model on top of its embeddings. That beats both hand-crafted radiomics and fine-tuning the same network end to end, and it recovers the same biology from mouse MRI and from human CT.

Telling early whether an immunotherapy is working

Journal of Translational Medicine (2024) and Diagnostics (2025).

Judging a liver-cancer treatment normally means waiting for the tumor to visibly change size, which is slow and sometimes misleading. These two papers predict the outcome from MRI taken earlier, first with radiomics and then with a single network that segments the tumor and predicts response together. Both are checked against the tissue rather than against a held-out split alone: the imaging measures track histology at r = 0.94, and predicted cell death lands within 0.013 RMSE of the stain.

When the reward teaches a model to stop thinking

Post-training Qwen3-VL with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards. Open source.

Rewarded purely on exact-match accuracy, the model learned to drop its chain-of-thought, keeping it in 17% of answers. That was the correct response to the incentive: reasoning makes the phrasing drift, and drift is scored as error. A small format reward brings the reasoning back at no cost to accuracy. The deeper problem was the grader itself, and a 19-cent audit found that between 23% and 49% of the answers it marked wrong were correct. Judged properly, the widely reported penalty for reasoning out loud mostly is not there.
