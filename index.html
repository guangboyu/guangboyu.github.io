About

I am a PhD candidate at UC Irvine, in Biomedical Engineering and Radiological Sciences. I work on foundation models and multimodal learning, usually where the data is small and the evaluation is the hard part.

Most of my work starts from the same constraint. Clinical datasets are limited, and the thing worth predicting, how a tumor is actually behaving, sits inside a biopsy. Imaging is the opposite: non-invasive, repeatable, and already part of standard care, but it shows anatomy rather than biology. My PhD is about closing that gap with models pretrained elsewhere, so that the biology is what gets learned here, not the representation.

A version of the same question comes up outside medicine: is the model using the image, or something easier? Post-training a vision-language model, I compared reinforcement learning against plain supervised fine-tuning on the same data. RL earns its place where reasoning pays, about ten points on chart questions, and its gains mostly vanish when the image is blacked out, as they should. Fine-tuning's partly survive: the model has learned answers it can give without looking. Applied to two 3D CT benchmarks, the same test showed that most of their published accuracy was reachable without the scan.

Both lines of work arrive at the same place. A number is worth what you checked it against, which is why the medical models are held against stained tissue and the language models against a blacked-out image.

Before the PhD I spent two years as an NLP engineer at Tencent QTrade, building named-entity models for a brokerage chat platform with more than 500,000 daily users. That is where I learned what a wrong prediction costs once it is serving live traffic. Away from research I photograph landscapes, mostly in the American West.

Reading tumor biology from a scan the patient already gets

Pancreatic cancer, mouse 9.4T MRI and patient CT. eBioMedicine, in press.

Reading a pancreatic tumor's immune and stromal makeup takes a biopsy today. We asked whether one imaging pipeline can read it from mouse MRI and from the CT patients already get.

AUROC per biomarker, each against its own ground truth (mouse · patient). Fine-tuning the same network end to end peaked at 0.63 and 0.70.

Mouse MRI, checked against whole-tumor histology, can nominate CT biomarkers that turn a routine scan into a noninvasive readout of tumor immunity and stroma.

Telling early whether an immunotherapy is working

Journal of Translational Medicine (2024) and Diagnostics (2025).

Judging a liver-cancer treatment normally means waiting for the tumor to visibly change size, which is slow and sometimes misleading. These two papers predict the outcome from MRI taken earlier, first with radiomics and then with a single network that segments the tumor and predicts response together. Both are checked against the tissue rather than against a held-out split alone: the imaging measures track histology at r = 0.94, and predicted cell death lands within 0.013 RMSE of the stain.

What reinforcement learning adds to a vision-language model

Qwen3-VL at 2B and 8B, supervised fine-tuning against RL from verifiable rewards, on photo, chart, and medical questions. Open source.

RL wins where reasoning pays: on chart questions it keeps its step-by-step reasoning and goes from 69.7% to 80.1%, about ten points above every short-answer model. Its gains also need the image. With a black image in place of the real one, the RL model answers no more questions than the base model in five of six settings, while supervised fine-tuning on the same questions answers up to 15 points more, having learned answers it can give without looking. Pointed at two 3D CT benchmarks, the same checks found most of their published accuracy reachable without the scan.
